The stand, front on

Five 800 mm bays under one mono-pitch roof. The bays are not arbitrary — each one solves a different problem, and the order they sit in is the order a customer meets them. Bays 03 and 04 split the vegetables by what they need rather than what they are, and bay 05 takes the fruit, which needs its own fittings. The bagged manure sits outside the stand entirely, on pallets.

02Section

Weather and reach

Two things decide whether a gate stand still looks good in year three: where the water goes, and whether a customer can reach the back of the bench without leaning on it.

Why these numbers

  • 700 mm deep, not 900. Any deeper and the back row becomes a dead zone that only you can reach — and things rot there.
  • 16° pitch. Well above the minimum for corrugate, and steep enough that leaves and blossom wash off instead of composting on your roof.
  • Walls on three sides. The back wall is the windbreak, the sign board, the punnet-rack and honesty-box fixing, and the reason rain never crosses the bench. Both ends close too — see Sheet 10 — but stop 200 mm short of the roof so the stand still breathes. Do not substitute a half wall anywhere.
  • Posts 600 mm into the ground with a concrete collar. A 4000 × 2050 stand with a solid back is a big sail. Nor’westers have taken lighter stands than this.
  • Eight posts. The pairs land on 0, 1600, 3200 and 4000, so no rail spans more than 1600 — which is what lets the lower shelf carry pumpkins and sacks of spuds without sagging.
03Site plan

Where it sits

Siting decides your takings more than anything you build. Two rules: customers must be able to stop safely, and they should never need to come through your gate.

Check before you form anything

The berm in front of your fence is almost always road reserve, not your land. Forming a metalled pull-off there, and putting a sign on it, usually needs your district council's say-so — and if you front a state highway, NZTA's. Ring the council roading team before you order metal; it is a five-minute call and it is much cheaper than moving it later.

If the road only allows a north-facing front, you have not lost — but extend the front overhang to 500 mm and add a drop-down shade batten across the top plate, and move the egg well to the shaded end of the bench.

04Detail

Eggs and herbs

Bay 02 works in two layers. Below the bench, two compartments that pull in opposite directions — keep eggs cool and shaded going out, keep returned cartons bone dry coming back — each under its own sloped, overhanging lid. Above it, at eye level and in the deepest shade the stand has, the herbs.

The egg well

Cut a well into the benchtop and drop a 20–30 L chilly bin into it, sitting on four cleats screwed to the bench frame so the rim carries the weight. You get insulation, a wipe-clean interior and a thing you can lift out and scrub, for about forty dollars. Size the opening to the bin's rim less 20 mm and cut it after you have the bin, not before.

Above it sits a sloped lid, 60 mm proud of the bench, overhanging its rebate on all four sides. Lift it, take a dozen, drop it. Nothing is on show to bake in the sun.

Do not chill them first

An egg taken out of a fridge and put in a warm stand sweats, and that condensation carries bacteria straight through the shell. Unwashed farm eggs are fine at ambient temperature — keep them shaded and steady, restock in small lots twice a day in summer, and never move them cold → warm.

The carton-return hatch

The compartment beside the well takes cartons back. This is the part people get wrong: they build a sealed box, and three weeks later it is full of soft, damp, unusable cardboard, because a closed box in the sun breathes moisture in and never lets it out. The box has to shed water and vent.

House rules for returns

  • Take back clean, dry, undamaged cartons only. Anything soft, stained or split goes in the fire, not back into stock.
  • Empty the return box every time you restock. Cartons that live in there for a fortnight are not worth reusing.
  • Reuse only cartons that carried your eggs, or cover the old producer's branding entirely with your own label — sending a buyer home with someone else's name and address on the box is misleading, and it is the one part of carton reuse that can actually get you in trouble.
  • Give people a reason: 20c off a dozen for a returned carton is enough, and it costs you less than a new carton does.
CARTON RETURN Clean & dry only, please 20c off your next dozen

The herb shelf

The 1400 mm shelf above the egg well is the best place on the stand for cut herbs, and it is worth being clear why: it is at eye level, it sits in the deepest shade the roof and back wall make, and it is directly above the honesty box — so the person already reaching for their wallet is looking straight at it.

The fitting is the bay 04 spouting detail, cut short and lifted: a 600 mm length of 150 mm galvanised spouting, stop-ends siliconed in, two dividers making three compartments, standing in 40–50 mm of water behind the shelf lip rail. Nothing new to design — it is Sheet 06, panel B, built shorter and raised 500 mm.

  • Coriander is the problem child. Cut, it is limp within hours and it is the classic “bought it, it was dead by dinner” complaint. Sell it rooted — pull the whole plant, wash the roots, stand it in the trough. It holds for days instead of hours, and the root itself is prized in Thai cooking, so say so on the label. It bolts in heat, so sow every three weeks.
  • Parsley is the tough one of the three and will stand all day happily. Flat-leaf outsells curly for cooking. Cut the outer stems and the plant keeps going for months.
  • Chives bunch with a rubber band and stand in water. When they flower, sell the purple pompoms too — they are edible, they garnish beautifully, and they cross-sell straight into the jar posies in bay 01.

There is now water directly above the eggs

The trough sits over the egg well and the carton return. Both are lidded, which covers most of it, but a knocked or overfilled trough runs down the back wall and into the bench. Three rules and it is a non-issue: silicone the stop-ends properly and leak-test it overnight on the ground before it ever goes up; fill to 50 mm and no further; and lift it down to change the water rather than pouring at head height over your own eggs.

Rosemary goes on the rail instead

Rosemary is woody and does not wilt, so putting it in water is pointless — and standing it with the soft herbs would only crowd the trough. It belongs on the bay 04 hanging rail, alongside the onions and the garlic, where the air moves and it slowly dries. Bunches hanging at head height, and a small dry bundle on the bench for people buying a couple of sprigs.

  • Cut long sprigs. They sell as roasting and barbecue skewers, which is worth more than the same weight cut short.
  • It is the winter herb. Evergreen, cropping every month of the year, and on the stand when the chives have died back and the coriander is months away.
  • It smells like the stand should. At 1750 mm, right where people reach past it.
CUT THIS MORNING Chives · Parsley · Coriander Coriander sold with its roots — it keeps for days
05Bay 01

Flowers, foliage and honey

Flowers are what stops the car. They go in the bay nearest the approaching traffic, at bench height where the colour is at eye level from the road. Honey sits on the shelf above them, a few jars at a time, because glass among glass suits both. Foliage stands in deep buckets below and in front — the least work and the longest life of anything on the stand.

The bucket board

Rather than standing buckets on the bench where a nor’wester will have them over, the benchtop in bay 01 is a bucket board: a 250 mm-deep piece of 18 mm ply let into the decking with two Ø230 mm holes at 260 mm centres, the third having given way to the herb trough. A tapered 10 L bucket drops in about 100 mm and wedges on its own taper — stable, quick to swap, and the water is out of sight.

  • Cut the holes with a jigsaw and a nail-and-string trammel; ease the edges with sandpaper so the buckets do not scuff.
  • Buckets are black, not white — less algae, and the stems read better against them.
  • Fill fresh every morning. Water more than a day old will cost you sales faster than any amount of arranging.

Agee jars on the upper shelf

Single-jar posies at 1400 mm are the impulse buy — someone who was not going to stop for a bunch will take a jar. Two details make the shelf work:

  • A 20 mm lip rail along the front edge of the shelf, so a knocked jar rocks back instead of off. Non-negotiable with a hard floor below.
  • Price the jar with the flowers and invite the jar back — a second return box is not needed; a crate marked JARS BACK HERE on the lower shelf does the job. Agee jars are worth more than the posy.

Foliage, below and in front

Foliage earns its bay space twice over: people arranging their own flowers need greenery, and a bunch of it turns a $5 bunch of blooms into something that looks like $15. It also outlasts everything else here — a bucket that does not sell today is still perfectly saleable next week, which almost nothing else on this stand can claim.

The buckets stand in front, not underneath. A foliage stem runs 600–800 mm and a 15 L bucket is 370 tall, so a full bucket stands about 770 — and there is only 600 mm of clear height under a 900 bench. They go instead in deep buckets on a low pallet plinth on the shingle, tight against the base of bay 01. Flowers above and behind, foliage below and in front, which is how a flower stall reads anyway.

  • 15 L buckets, not the 10 L used above. Woody stems drink hard and long stems need the ballast or the first gust has them over. Half-fill them.
  • Split or crush every woody stem end before it goes in, and strip any leaf below the waterline. It is the difference between one week and three.
  • Cut in the morning and condition in deep water for a few hours in the shade before anything goes out.

The three worth growing for it

  • Eucalyptus is the workhorse — silver dollar and cinerea both sell. Scented, three weeks in water, and it dries well, so stems that do not sell become dried bunches rather than waste. Cut it in the cool of the morning; leaves picked in afternoon heat droop and never fully come back.
  • Olive is the one people ask for by name, especially for weddings — grey-green, elegant, and a very long vase life. Cut sparingly from any one tree; olives are slow and you will see the shape change before the tree minds.
  • Hydrangea is the odd one out and worth understanding. It is not really foliage, and it wilts faster than anything else you will cut, because it drinks through its head as well as its stem. Cut it, split the stem, and submerge the whole head in water for an hour before it goes out. A wilted head will usually come back from a full submersion — tell customers that, because most of them have written hydrangeas off. Heads left to go papery on the bush in late season sell again as dried hydrangea right through winter.

Foliage is what keeps bay 01 open in July

Eucalyptus and olive crop every month of the year, and dried hydrangea and dried gum carry straight through the cold. When the flowers stop in May this bay would otherwise be three empty buckets and some honey. Put a board up saying the foliage is there — nobody expects to buy greenery at a gate stall, so nobody looks for it unless you tell them.

Honey among the posies

Two or three jars of honey set between the Agee jars on the bay 01 shelf, not a row of a dozen on its own. It is the same glass at the same scale, the amber reads beautifully against cut flowers, and it puts the highest-value jar on the stand in the first bay a customer reaches — where they are still browsing rather than counting out money.

  • A few jars, restocked from the house. Honey is worth more per unit than anything else here. Put out what you would be relaxed about losing and bring more down when they go.
  • The lip rail is already there for the Agee jars, and honey needs it just as much. Never stack jars, and never let sun reach them — heat and light darken honey and strip the flavour, and the same jar in a bright spot is a visibly worse product in a fortnight.
  • Two sizes, whole dollars. 500 g and 1 kg, tamper-evident lids, and check every seal before it goes out.
  • Crystallised honey is not spoiled honey, but most customers think it is. A small card — “Gone solid? That is real honey doing what it does. Stand the jar in warm water.” — sells more of it than anything else you can write.

Honey jars do not come back

You have deliberately built a returns culture for egg cartons, and Agee jars come back for the flowers — which makes this bay exactly where the confusion will happen. Say plainly on the shelf that honey jars are not returnable. Refilling a customer’s used jar with food is a different proposition entirely from reusing a dry carton or a rinsed flower jar, and it is very unlikely to be acceptable under whatever food programme you end up operating.

OUR OWN HONEY 500 g and 1 kg · raw and unfiltered Gone solid? Stand the jar in warm water

What actually sells at a gate

Mixed seasonal bunches at one flat price beat carefully graded ranges every time. Wrap in newspaper or brown paper with a rubber band, not cellophane. Keep the buckets full but not crammed — a bucket with six good bunches in it sells better than one with fifteen.

06Bays 03–04

Vegetables

Cucumbers, courgettes, rocket, radishes, beetroot, carrots, parsnips, leeks, broccoli, cauliflower and punnets of beans, peas, cherry tomatoes, strawberries, raspberries and redcurrants will not fit in one bay — and they should not share one anyway. They split cleanly in two by how they have to be handled: small and perishable on a raked rack in the cool, bunched and heavy standing in water or sitting low.

Bay 03 — punnets and greens

Two problems, one bay. Punnets are light, high-margin and invisible lying flat; brassicas and cucumbers are the most heat-sensitive things you will ever put out. The rack solves the first, the well solves the second, and neither takes bench space from the other.

  • The punnet rack takes beans, peas, cherry tomatoes and every berry you grow. Three tiers of 18 mm ply, each 800 × 200, raked about 15° toward the customer with a 30 mm lip along the front edge so nothing slides off. Tiers at 980, 1120 and 1260 — a 140 mm rise, which clears a standard punnet with room to lift it out. Screwed through into the back-wall studs, not the ply.
  • The greens well is the egg well built a second time (Sheet 04, refs E1–E2) — another 20–30 L chilly bin dropped into the bench under its own sloped lid. Broccoli, cauliflower, cucumbers, courgettes, rocket, lettuce and bunched radishes go in under a damp cloth. It is the single biggest thing you can do for your afternoon takings in February.
  • Leave the leaves on the cauliflower. They are the packaging — they keep the curd white and unbruised, and a trimmed cauli in the sun yellows in an hour.
  • Cucumbers and courgettes go in one layer in a shallow crate, never stacked. They bruise where they touch and they roll off anything flat. Pick courgettes small and often — a marrow that got away sells to nobody.
  • Rocket wilts faster than anything else here. Cut it in the dark, bunch it damp, and keep it in the well. If it is limp by ten in the morning you cut it too late.

Bay 04 — bunches and bulk

Everything here is either bunched with its tops on or heavy enough that it belongs near the ground. The lower-shelf rails in this bay step up to 140 × 45 for that reason — three pumpkins and a couple of 20 kg sacks is real load over a 1600 mm span.

  • The root trough takes carrots, parsnips, beetroot, leeks and bunched radishes, tied at the shoulder with tops on and standing in shallow water. Change the water every morning; a trough of yesterday's water will cost you more sales than an empty bench.
  • Beetroot sells on its tops. Leave 50 mm of stalk and the young leaves on — they are the freshness signal, and half your buyers will cook them. Never trim the root itself or it bleeds out.
  • Parsnips are a winter line. They sweeten after frost, so do not put them out too early — and pull any that have gone woody and oversized, because one of those loses you the customer for good.
  • Leeks carry grit down in the shank. Rinse and trim the roots before bunching, or the first person to cook one will not come back.
  • Carrots both ways: bunched with tops in the trough at a premium, and loose in a crate beside it for people who just want a feed. The tops wilt well before the root does, so move the bunched ones first.
  • Onions and garlic on the rail above, plaited and cured hard. Pumpkins and potato sacks below — stalks on, paper or hessian, never clear plastic.

The quiet win in this list

Most of what you have added is cool-season. Parsnips, leeks, broccoli and cauliflower come into their own from about April through September — exactly the months when the eggs slow down, the flowers stop and a three-bay stand would have been carrying pumpkins and spuds alone. Bays 03 and 04 do not just add summer capacity; they give the stand a reason to stay open all winter.

07Bay 05

Orchard

Fruit is the first thing on this list that could not be squeezed into an existing bay. Apples keep for months and shrug off handling; a nectarine bruises if you look at it wrong. They need the same bay and completely different fittings — and the fitting stone fruit needs is the opposite of the one that works for punnets.

Three fruits, three treatments

  • Peaches and nectarines are the most fragile thing on this stand, ahead of the eggs. One layer, never stacked, handled once. Set them out and do not touch them again — every pick-up-and-put-down is a bruise that shows up brown two days later. Slatted trays so air moves under them.
  • Apples are the opposite — robust, heavy, and they keep. Loose in a crate on the bench for people picking a few, and by the box on the lower shelf for people who want a bin of them for cooking. Mark the variety and whether they are cookers or eaters; it is the question you will otherwise be asked every time.
  • Strawberries go in punnets on the bay 03 rack with the other punnets, not here. They want the rake and the shade more than they want to be filed under fruit.

Keep the apples out of the greens well

Apples give off ethylene as they ripen. In a closed insulated bin it will yellow your rocket and lettuce and turn carrots bitter within a couple of days. Never put apples in the greens well or in any sealed container with leaves or roots. In the open air of the stand, with a post between the bays and stock that turns over daily, the effect is small — it is enclosure that does the damage, not proximity.

Building the tray rack

  • Three slatted trays, each 800 × 250, at the same 980 / 1120 / 1260 levels as the punnet rack so the two bays read as one system from the road.
  • Slats, not solid ply. 40 × 20 on 15 mm gaps. Air under the fruit, and any drip or rain blown in goes straight through instead of sitting under a peach.
  • 5° only, falling forward, and a 30 mm lip — enough that a knocked tray does not tip its load onto the bench, not enough to make the fruit roll.
  • Line the trays with newspaper and change it daily. It is free, it cushions, and it makes a bruised fruit obvious the moment it marks the paper.

Stone fruit is a three-week business

Peaches and nectarines come and go in a rush — roughly January to March, with any one variety giving you two or three good weeks. Price to clear the tray daily rather than holding stock back; a peach held over is worth nothing tomorrow. Apples are the reverse and will carry bay 05 from March right through winter.

08Off-stand

Pallets and bulk

One rule covers everything that will not fit on a shelf: nothing sits on the shingle. Not a sack, not a pumpkin, not a bucket. Shingle wicks water straight up into a sack, grit works into everything standing on it, and anything at ground level is picked up from a full bend. Recycled pallets fix all three for nothing, and the stand uses three of them.

Why two heights

The manure plinth is two pallets because you lift 20 kg sacks off it repeatedly and the extra 145 mm changes the whole lift. The two food plinths are one pallet because they sit where customers stand: a 290 mm step at the foot of the counter is something people trip over while reaching across it. Keep both no more than 500 mm deep so nobody has to step onto one, tuck them under the 300 mm roof overhang, and paint the front edge white so it is seen.

The lower shelf still does the work

Pumpkins and sacks on display stay on the slatted lower shelf at 300 — already off the ground, already under the roof. The bulk plinth is for the overflow: the spare half-dozen pumpkins and the stack of sacks you would otherwise stand on the gravel.

The manure stack

Bagged farm manure does not belong in the stand, and building it a bay was over-engineering. It needs no roof, no gate and no insulated separation — it needs to be off the ground, at a height a person can lift from, and well clear of the food. Two stacked pallets do all of that for nothing.

Building it

  • Two pallets, not one. One gets you 145 mm, which is barely worth the trouble. Two gets you to 290 and the whole lift changes.
  • Turn the top one 90° and screw down through it into the bottom pallet's blocks. Loose stacked pallets skid apart, and a skidding pallet with 200 kg on it is how people get hurt.
  • Heat-treated pallets only. Check the ISPM 15 stamp on the block: HT is heat treated and fine; MB is methyl bromide and has no place near food or a vegetable garden. Free pallets are everywhere — there is no reason to take a bad one.
  • Free-draining metal underneath, graded to fall away. A bottom pallet standing in water rots out in a season and stains everything above it.
  • Set it 1500 mm minimum clear of bay 05, and downhill and downwind of the food bays if the site gives you the choice. Manure is raw animal waste; it does not need a cage, but it does need distance.
  • Keep the hand sanitiser on the bay-05 post. The person who just carried a manure bag is the next one picking up your lettuce.

Bagging and running it

  • 15–20 kg a bag, and keep it consistent. This is the single best thing you can do for sales. A 25 kg bag is a two-person lift for a lot of buyers, and they will walk away rather than say so.
  • Heavy woven poly or plastic bags with a tied neck and a few slits cut in the base, so any rain that gets in drains out. Paper sacks are for undercover only — outdoors they turn to pulp in one wet week.
  • Three bags high, no more. Beyond that you are lifting above shoulder height to build the stack, which defeats the point of the pallets.
  • Say what it is on the sign: what animal, and whether it is raw or composted. Gardeners care a great deal about the difference and will ask.

It costs you nothing and sells all winter

This is the only line on the stand made entirely from something you already have to deal with. It needs no water, no shade and no daily attention, it does not wilt, and it sells hardest in August to October when people are digging beds over — the back half of your lean season.

09Detail

Getting paid

An honesty box works in New Zealand — but it works far better when it is obviously hard to rob and when there is a second way to pay. Build both in from day one.

Specification

  • Shell: 3 mm folded steel, or 18 mm ply lined with sheet steel on the door and floor. Powder-coat or paint to match the stand.
  • Slot: 120 × 12 mm in the front face, under a small hood so rain cannot run in. Coins and folded notes go through; a hand does not.
  • Baffles: two 2 mm plates at roughly 45°, staggered so there is no straight path from the slot to the cash.
  • Lock: cam lock or a hasp and a decent padlock. Two keys, one on the tractor.
  • Fixing: 4 × M8 coach bolts through the centre stud, nuts and washers inside the locked cavity.
  • Mounted at 1150–1350 mm on the back wall of bay 02, so it is at hand height and directly in front of anyone taking eggs.

The second way to pay

Bank transfer now does more of the work than the box does. Put your account number on a painted sign at eye level with a short reference instruction, and add a QR code that opens a pre-filled payment link. It removes the change problem entirely, it gives you a record for your books, and it means a customer with no cash still buys.

HONESTY BOX Cash in the slot, or bank transfer 00-0000-0000000-00 · ref: your name

Keeping the box boring to rob

  • Empty it daily, and put up a small sign saying you do. An obviously empty box is not worth breaking.
  • No float. Price everything in whole dollars — $5, $8, $10 — so nobody needs change and there is never a dish of coins on show.
  • Keep a tally book in the stand, or note the takings when you empty it. Gate sales are still income, and a shoebox of receipts in April is nobody's idea of a good time.
  • A camera on the shed pointed at the stand does more than a bigger padlock, and a small sign saying so does more again.
10Shelter

Walls and signs

Closing both ends turns the stand from a roof into a shelter — and wind, not rain, is what actually wrecks cut flowers and herbs. But a fully boxed-in stand stops the airflow the hanging garlic and rosemary need, so the ends close with a gap left deliberately at the top.

Why close the ends at all

  • Wind, not rain, is what ruins the stock. A nor’wester blowing the length of an open-ended stand strips cut flowers, dries out herbs and lifts anything light straight off the bench. The roof already handles the rain.
  • Two more sign faces, one of them facing the traffic before it reaches you — the single most valuable surface on the whole build.
  • The end bays stop being second-class. Bay 01 and bay 05 are currently the exposed ones; closing the ends means the flowers and the stone fruit get the same shelter as the middle.
  • It costs two sheets of ply and two studs, and the frame and bracing are already there to carry it.

Signs that change without a screwdriver

What the stand sells changes every few weeks, so painting the bay names on the wall would be wrong within a month. Instead the fascia of the upper shelf carries a grooved rail the full 4000, and the signs are loose boards that drop into it. Finish the boards in blackboard paint and the two ideas become one: a permanent line gets hand-lettered once, a changing line gets chalked and wiped.

The boards

  • Make a dozen at once. 6 mm ply, 380 × 180, edges sealed, blackboard paint both sides. Half a sheet does the lot and you will want spares.
  • Hand-letter the lines that never change — EGGS, HONEY, FLOWERS — and chalk the rest. Two boards per bay if you want a name and a price.
  • All chalk lives under the roof. The 300 mm front overhang keeps the rail dry; anything chalked on an outside face is gone in the first southerly. The end walls get paint, not chalk.
  • Keep a fat chalk marker in the stand, not a chalk stick. It survives damp air, and it wipes off with a wet cloth when you want it to.
  • Turn a board blank-side out when a line runs out, rather than leaving a sign for something you have not got. Nothing costs you a repeat customer faster.

12Sequence

Build sequence

Two weekends, in this order. The boxes are bench work — build them inside on a wet evening while the concrete goes off.

  1. Set out. String line along the fence, mark the eight post centres on a 4000 × 700 rectangle with pairs at 0, 1600, 3200 and 4000, and check the diagonals match before you dig anything. Confirm the boundary while you are at it.
  2. Dig and set. Eight holes 600 deep, 300 across. Bed 100 mm of compacted metal in the base of each so the post foot drains, stand the posts, brace them plumb and pour the collars. Leave them overnight.
  3. Top plates and braces. Front plate at 2050, rear at 1850, bolted through with M12s. Fit the eight diagonal braces in the back frame now — the stand will feel completely different once they are in.
  4. Bench and shelf frames. Bench rails at 900, the deeper 140 × 45 shelf rails at 300, joists at 640 centres between them. Frame the three wells — egg, carton return and greens — while the frame is still open. Retro-fitting them later is miserable.
  5. Rafters, purlins, roof. Birdsmouth the six rafters over both plates at 800 centres, 300 proud at the front and 100 at the rear. Three purlins, then the five corrugate sheets, fixed on the crest with EPDM-washered screws. Never fix in the pan.
  6. Back wall and both end walls. Ply on, studs at 800, 1200 and 2400, every sheet joint landing on a stud; the end walls take a mid stud each and stop 200 mm below the plate, with bird mesh stapled across the strip from inside. All edges primed and sealed — especially the bottom edge, which is what rots first. Hold it 50 mm clear of the ground.
  7. Decking. Bench and shelves the full 4000, 5 mm gaps between boards. Cut the two well openings and the bucket-board recess after the decking is down and you have both bins in your hand.
  8. Boxes. Glue and screw the return box with exterior PU, seal every cut edge, fit hinge, seal, vents, battens and weep holes. Drop it into its compartment and hang all three lids.
  9. Fit-out. Punnet rack screwed through into the bay-03 studs and the orchard tray rack into the bay-05 studs — check the angles before you fix them, since one rakes 15° and the other only 5°. Hanging rail flanges under the front plate in bay 04, both troughs with their stop-ends siliconed and leak-tested on the ground overnight — the herb one goes up on the bay 02 shelf, over your own eggs, so do not skip that — shelf lip rails in bays 01 and 02, the two grooved sign rails run the full length, honesty box bolted to the 1200 stud, signs on.
  10. The three pallet plinths. Grade free-draining metal under each. Cut-down single pallets tight to the base of bays 01 and 04 for the foliage buckets and the bulk produce, front edges painted white; two heat-treated pallets stacked and screwed together, top one turned 90°, sited 1500 mm clear of bay 05 for the manure. Bracket the hand sanitiser to the bay-05 post. Do the manure one last — nothing about it should touch the food bays.
  11. Paint. Primer plus two coats, end grain first. Leave it a fortnight before you seal the return box's rubber against fresh paint.
  12. Soft open. One weekend with eggs and one other line only. Watch what people actually stop for, then load the bays properly.

The detail everyone skips

Seal every cut end and every drilled hole, on the treated timber and the ply both. Treatment does not penetrate to the core, so a raw cut end standing in a puddle is the one place a stand like this fails, and it fails from the inside where you cannot see it coming.

13Compliance

Before you open

Selling your own eggs and produce at your own gate is deliberately easy in New Zealand — but the detail sits with MPI and your council, and it does change. Treat everything below as the list of things to confirm, not as the answer.

Confirm these yourself

Rules and thresholds shift, and yours may differ by district. Ring MPI about the eggs and your district council about the stall and the sign before you open. Both calls are short, free, and much easier than being asked to shut down in your second week.

Eggs

  • MPI has long allowed small flocks selling direct to consumers at the gate to operate without a registered risk management programme — the commonly cited line is 100 or fewer laying hens. Confirm the current threshold and what it requires of you.
  • Never sell cracked, leaking or visibly dirty eggs. This is the one rule with no nuance in it.
  • Do not wash them. Washing strips the bloom that keeps bacteria out. Wipe a lightly marked egg dry; discard a badly soiled one.
  • Label each carton with your name, your address and the pack date, even where you are not strictly required to. It is good practice, it builds trust, and it makes a traceback trivial if you ever need one.
  • Reused cartons must not carry another producer's branding in a way that misleads the buyer. Your own label over the top solves it.

Honey — the strictest thing on this stand

Do not treat honey like eggs. It is comfortably the most regulated line here, and the gap is wide enough that it is worth a conversation with MPI before you extract a single frame for sale.

  • Register your hives. Beekeeping in New Zealand sits under the American Foulbrood National Pest Management Plan — apiaries have to be registered and an annual disease return filed. This applies whether or not you sell a thing.
  • Honey is an animal product. Extracting and packing it for sale generally has to happen under a registered risk management programme. There are small-scale pathways for selling direct to consumers, but they are not automatic the way the small-flock egg position is. Confirm which one you fall under before you start.
  • Label it properly: your name and address, net weight, a batch or best-before date. Treat this as required, not optional.
  • Do not write “mānuka” unless the honey has been tested and meets MPI’s definition. It is a legally defined term with a specific test behind it, and it is the single easiest way to get yourself in real trouble on a $12 jar.
  • No health or therapeutic claims. Not on the jar, not on the sign, not on a chalkboard.
  • Carry the infant warning: honey is not for children under 12 months.

Fruit and vegetables

  • Whole, unprocessed produce sold by the grower generally sits outside the Food Act's registration requirements. The moment you cut, cook, bag as a prepared salad, or preserve anything, you are in different territory — check first.
  • Sell your own produce. Reselling someone else's changes what you are, both legally and to the neighbours.

Manure

  • Age or compost it before you bag it. Raw manure carries E. coli, salmonella and campylobacter, and a good share of your buyers will put it straight on a salad bed. Say clearly on the sign whether it is raw or well rotted, and give people the raw stuff only if they ask for it.
  • Check it for residual herbicide — this is the one that will come back on you. Aminopyralid and clopyralid, used on pasture and carried in bought-in hay, survive the animal and the compost heap, and will deform and kill tomatoes, beans, peas and potatoes in a customer’s garden months later. If your pasture or your hay has been sprayed with either, do not sell the manure. If you are not certain, do the pot test below first.
  • The bean test. Pot up a few bean or pea seedlings in a 50/50 mix of your manure and clean potting mix, and the same number in clean mix alone. Three weeks. If the manure-grown ones come up cupped, fern-like or twisted, you have a herbicide problem and the whole heap is unsellable.
  • Composting also kills weed seed, which is the other thing that will lose you a repeat customer.
  • Bagged manure sold as a soil conditioner may fall under New Zealand’s fertiliser labelling requirements. Straight animal manures are usually treated more lightly than compound fertilisers — confirm where yours sits with MPI before you print a label.
  • Never move manure off a property under a biosecurity movement restriction.

The road and the sign

  • The berm is usually road reserve. A formed pull-off, and often a roadside sign, needs council approval; on a state highway it needs NZTA's.
  • Some district plans treat a permanent roadside stall as a structure or as commercial activity in a rural zone. Ask before you concrete anything in.
  • Sightlines both ways matter more than any of it. If a car cannot stop safely, the stand is in the wrong place regardless of what the rules permit.

Money and cover

  • Gate takings are income. Keep a tally book in the stand; it takes ten seconds a day and saves a bad afternoon in April.
  • Tell your insurer. A structure at your boundary that the public uses is worth a sentence on the policy, and public liability cover is usually cheap to add.
14Operating

Running it

The stand is a fortnight's work. What decides whether it is still going next summer is the rhythm you put around it.

Price so nobody needs change

Whole dollars, ideally $5 / $8 / $10, and no odd cents anywhere on the board. It removes the float, it removes the coin dish, and it removes the customer who drives off because they only had a twenty. Hand-letter the prices on a board rather than printing them — painted prices read as a farm, printed ones read as a shop.

Restock twice, not once

Morning and late afternoon in summer, once a day the rest of the year. Every restock: pull anything tired, empty the carton return, empty the honesty box, top up the flower buckets, write the day's takings in the book. Ten minutes, and it is the difference between a stand people check and one they drive past.

Never leave it empty-looking

A half-empty bay reads as closed from a moving car. If a line runs out, spread the rest across the bench and put a jar of flowers where the gap is. Keep one thing at eye level in every bay at all times.

Signs to paint

The approach end wall

EGGS · FLOWERS · VEG
Painted, not chalked. Big, high-contrast, one line, readable at 80 km/h. Still not a list — a dozen lines on one roadside sign reads as nothing at all.

On the back wall

Everything here is grown, laid or collected on this farm.
One sentence. With the bought-in fertiliser gone, it is true again — the manure is yours too.

By the box

Cash in the slot or bank transfer.
Account number, reference, QR. Emptied daily.

On the hatches

EGGS — lift lid and CARTON RETURN — clean & dry. People will not open an unlabelled box.

What to change after the first season

  • Watch which bay empties first and give it more room. The five-bay split is a starting hypothesis, not a finding — if the punnets outrun everything, the rack wants a fourth tier or a second bay.
  • Count the carton returns. If the rate is under about a third, the incentive is too small or the box is not obvious enough — usually the second.
  • If cash keeps beating transfers, your account sign is too small or too far from the goods. Move it to eye level directly above the egg well.
  • Foliage is the least work on the stand. It needs no shade, no daily pull-through and no restocking rhythm, and it keeps for weeks. If a bucket is slow, leave it — unlike everything else here, waiting costs you nothing.
  • Herbs are the best margin on the stand. If the trough empties daily, widen it before you widen anything else — a second 250 mm length costs almost nothing and takes only the last flower bucket.
  • Use the sign rail properly. A board chalked with what came in this morning is the cheapest thing you can do to make regulars stop again — and turning a board blank-side out the moment a line runs out is the cheapest way to stop them driving off disappointed.
  • Five bays is about right for one run. At 4000 the stand reads as a whole from a moving car and every bay is within an easy restock walk. Anything that does not need a roof — the manure is the example — should sit outside it on its own rather than driving the building longer.
  • If the manure pays, it is the easiest line to grow — pea straw, bagged compost and seed potatoes need no refrigeration, no water and no daily attention, and the same pallet stack carries all of them straight through the winter.
