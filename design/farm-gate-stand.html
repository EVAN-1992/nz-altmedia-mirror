01Elevation

The stand, front on

Four 800 mm bays under one mono-pitch roof. The bays are not arbitrary — each one solves a different problem, and the order they sit in is the order a customer meets them. Bays 03 and 04 split the vegetables by what they need, not by what they are: small and soft on a raked rack, bunched and heavy standing in water or sitting low.

02Section

Weather and reach

Two things decide whether a gate stand still looks good in year three: where the water goes, and whether a customer can reach the back of the bench without leaning on it.

Why these numbers

  • 700 mm deep, not 900. Any deeper and the back row becomes a dead zone that only you can reach — and things rot there.
  • 16° pitch. Well above the minimum for corrugate, and steep enough that leaves and blossom wash off instead of composting on your roof.
  • Solid back wall, full height. It is the windbreak, the sign board, the punnet-rack and honesty-box fixing, and the reason rain never crosses the bench. Do not substitute a half wall.
  • Posts 600 mm into the ground with a concrete collar. A 3200 × 2050 stand with a solid back is a big sail. Nor’westers have taken lighter stands than this.
  • Six posts, not four. Widening to 3200 puts a third pair on the 1600 line, so no rail spans more than 1600 — which is what lets the lower shelf carry pumpkins and 20 kg sacks without sagging.
03Site plan

Where it sits

Siting decides your takings more than anything you build. Two rules: customers must be able to stop safely, and they should never need to come through your gate.

Check before you form anything

The berm in front of your fence is almost always road reserve, not your land. Forming a metalled pull-off there, and putting a sign on it, usually needs your district council's say-so — and if you front a state highway, NZTA's. Ring the council roading team before you order metal; it is a five-minute call and it is much cheaper than moving it later.

If the road only allows a north-facing front, you have not lost — but extend the front overhang to 500 mm and add a drop-down shade batten across the top plate, and move the egg well to the shaded end of the bench.

04Detail

The egg station

Bay 02 does two jobs that pull in opposite directions: keep eggs cool and shaded going out, and keep returned cartons bone dry coming back. Both become compartments inside the bench, each under its own sloped, overhanging lid.

The egg well

Cut a well into the benchtop and drop a 20–30 L chilly bin into it, sitting on four cleats screwed to the bench frame so the rim carries the weight. You get insulation, a wipe-clean interior and a thing you can lift out and scrub, for about forty dollars. Size the opening to the bin's rim less 20 mm and cut it after you have the bin, not before.

Above it sits a sloped lid, 60 mm proud of the bench, overhanging its rebate on all four sides. Lift it, take a dozen, drop it. Nothing is on show to bake in the sun.

Do not chill them first

An egg taken out of a fridge and put in a warm stand sweats, and that condensation carries bacteria straight through the shell. Unwashed farm eggs are fine at ambient temperature — keep them shaded and steady, restock in small lots twice a day in summer, and never move them cold → warm.

The carton-return hatch

The compartment beside the well takes cartons back. This is the part people get wrong: they build a sealed box, and three weeks later it is full of soft, damp, unusable cardboard, because a closed box in the sun breathes moisture in and never lets it out. The box has to shed water and vent.

House rules for returns

  • Take back clean, dry, undamaged cartons only. Anything soft, stained or split goes in the fire, not back into stock.
  • Empty the return box every time you restock. Cartons that live in there for a fortnight are not worth reusing.
  • Reuse only cartons that carried your eggs, or cover the old producer's branding entirely with your own label — sending a buyer home with someone else's name and address on the box is misleading, and it is the one part of carton reuse that can actually get you in trouble.
  • Give people a reason: 20c off a dozen for a returned carton is enough, and it costs you less than a new carton does.
CARTON RETURN Clean & dry only, please 20c off your next dozen
05Bay 01

Flowers

Flowers are what stops the car. They go in the bay nearest the approaching traffic, and they go at bench height where the colour is at eye level from the road.

The bucket board

Rather than standing buckets on the bench where a nor’wester will have them over, the benchtop in bay 01 is a bucket board: a 250 mm-deep piece of 18 mm ply let into the decking with three Ø230 mm holes at 260 mm centres. A tapered 10 L bucket drops in about 100 mm and wedges on its own taper — stable, quick to swap, and the water is out of sight.

  • Cut the holes with a jigsaw and a nail-and-string trammel; ease the edges with sandpaper so the buckets do not scuff.
  • Buckets are black, not white — less algae, and the stems read better against them.
  • Fill fresh every morning. Water more than a day old will cost you sales faster than any amount of arranging.

Agee jars on the upper shelf

Single-jar posies at 1400 mm are the impulse buy — someone who was not going to stop for a bunch will take a jar. Two details make the shelf work:

  • A 20 mm lip rail along the front edge of the shelf, so a knocked jar rocks back instead of off. Non-negotiable with a hard floor below.
  • Price the jar with the flowers and invite the jar back — a second return box is not needed; a crate marked JARS BACK HERE on the lower shelf does the job. Agee jars are worth more than the posy.

What actually sells at a gate

Mixed seasonal bunches at one flat price beat carefully graded ranges every time. Wrap in newspaper or brown paper with a rubber band, not cellophane. Keep the buckets full but not crammed — a bucket with six good bunches in it sells better than one with fifteen.

06Bays 03–04

Vegetables

Cucumbers, carrots, parsnips, leeks, broccoli, cauliflower and punnets of beans, peas and cherry tomatoes will not fit in one bay — and they should not share one anyway. They split cleanly in two by how they have to be handled: small and perishable on a raked rack in the cool, bunched and heavy standing in water or sitting low.

Bay 03 — punnets and greens

Two problems, one bay. Punnets are light, high-margin and invisible lying flat; brassicas and cucumbers are the most heat-sensitive things you will ever put out. The rack solves the first, the well solves the second, and neither takes bench space from the other.

  • The punnet rack is three tiers of 18 mm ply, each 800 × 200, raked about 15° toward the customer with a 30 mm lip along the front edge so nothing slides off. Tiers at 980, 1120 and 1260 — a 140 mm rise, which clears a standard punnet with room to lift it out. Screwed through into the back-wall studs, not the ply.
  • The greens well is the egg well built a second time (Sheet 04, refs E1–E2) — another 20–30 L chilly bin dropped into the bench under its own sloped lid. Broccoli, cauliflower, cucumbers and lettuce go in under a damp cloth. It is the single biggest thing you can do for your afternoon takings in February.
  • Leave the leaves on the cauliflower. They are the packaging — they keep the curd white and unbruised, and a trimmed cauli in the sun yellows in an hour.
  • Cucumbers go in one layer in a shallow crate, never stacked. They bruise where they touch and they roll off anything flat.

Bay 04 — bunches and bulk

Everything here is either bunched with its tops on or heavy enough that it belongs near the ground. The lower-shelf rails in this bay step up to 140 × 45 for that reason — three pumpkins and a couple of 20 kg sacks is real load over a 1600 mm span.

  • The root trough takes carrots, parsnips and leeks bunched at the shoulder with tops on, standing in shallow water. Change the water every morning; a trough of yesterday's water will cost you more sales than an empty bench.
  • Parsnips are a winter line. They sweeten after frost, so do not put them out too early — and pull any that have gone woody and oversized, because one of those loses you the customer for good.
  • Leeks carry grit down in the shank. Rinse and trim the roots before bunching, or the first person to cook one will not come back.
  • Carrots both ways: bunched with tops in the trough at a premium, and loose in a crate beside it for people who just want a feed. The tops wilt well before the root does, so move the bunched ones first.
  • Onions and garlic on the rail above, plaited and cured hard. Pumpkins and potato sacks below — stalks on, paper or hessian, never clear plastic.

The quiet win in this list

Most of what you have added is cool-season. Parsnips, leeks, broccoli and cauliflower come into their own from about April through September — exactly the months when the eggs slow down, the flowers stop and a three-bay stand would have been carrying pumpkins and spuds alone. Bays 03 and 04 do not just add summer capacity; they give the stand a reason to stay open all winter.

07Detail

Getting paid

An honesty box works in New Zealand — but it works far better when it is obviously hard to rob and when there is a second way to pay. Build both in from day one.

Specification

  • Shell: 3 mm folded steel, or 18 mm ply lined with sheet steel on the door and floor. Powder-coat or paint to match the stand.
  • Slot: 120 × 12 mm in the front face, under a small hood so rain cannot run in. Coins and folded notes go through; a hand does not.
  • Baffles: two 2 mm plates at roughly 45°, staggered so there is no straight path from the slot to the cash.
  • Lock: cam lock or a hasp and a decent padlock. Two keys, one on the tractor.
  • Fixing: 4 × M8 coach bolts through the centre stud, nuts and washers inside the locked cavity.
  • Mounted at 1150–1350 mm on the back wall of bay 02, so it is at hand height and directly in front of anyone taking eggs.

The second way to pay

Bank transfer now does more of the work than the box does. Put your account number on a painted sign at eye level with a short reference instruction, and add a QR code that opens a pre-filled payment link. It removes the change problem entirely, it gives you a record for your books, and it means a customer with no cash still buys.

HONESTY BOX Cash in the slot, or bank transfer 00-0000-0000000-00 · ref: your name

Keeping the box boring to rob

  • Empty it daily, and put up a small sign saying you do. An obviously empty box is not worth breaking.
  • No float. Price everything in whole dollars — $5, $8, $10 — so nobody needs change and there is never a dish of coins on show.
  • Keep a tally book in the stand, or note the takings when you empty it. Gate sales are still income, and a shoebox of receipts in April is nobody's idea of a good time.
  • A camera on the shed pointed at the stand does more than a bigger padlock, and a small sign saying so does more again.

On the estimate

At 2026 New Zealand retail this list lands roughly between $1,250 and $1,700 — about $300 more than the three-bay version, most of it the extra 800 mm of timber and roof, the second chilly bin and the heavier lower-shelf rails. Milled macrocarpa off the farm, recycled iron, or a second-hand cash box will take a solid few hundred off. Prices move — treat this as a shopping list to price, not a quote.

09Sequence

Build sequence

Two weekends, in this order. The boxes are bench work — build them inside on a wet evening while the concrete goes off.

  1. Set out. String line along the fence, mark the six post centres on a 3200 × 700 rectangle with the middle pair at 1600, and check the diagonals match before you dig anything. Confirm the boundary while you are at it.
  2. Dig and set. Six holes 600 deep, 300 across. Bed 100 mm of compacted metal in the base of each so the post foot drains, stand the posts, brace them plumb and pour the collars. Leave them overnight.
  3. Top plates and braces. Front plate at 2050, rear at 1850, bolted through with M12s. Fit the six diagonal braces in the back frame now — the stand will feel completely different once they are in.
  4. Bench and shelf frames. Bench rails at 900, the deeper 140 × 45 shelf rails at 300, joists at 640 centres between them. Frame the three wells — egg, carton return and greens — while the frame is still open. Retro-fitting them later is miserable.
  5. Rafters, purlins, roof. Birdsmouth the five rafters over both plates at 800 centres, 300 proud at the front and 100 at the rear. Three purlins, then the four corrugate sheets, fixed on the crest with EPDM-washered screws. Never fix in the pan.
  6. Back wall. Ply on, studs at 800, 1200 and 2400, every sheet joint landing on a stud. All edges primed and sealed — especially the bottom edge, which is what rots first. Hold it 50 mm clear of the ground.
  7. Decking. Bench and shelves, 5 mm gaps between boards. Cut the two well openings and the bucket-board recess after the decking is down and you have both bins in your hand.
  8. Boxes. Glue and screw the return box with exterior PU, seal every cut edge, fit hinge, seal, vents, battens and weep holes. Drop it into its compartment and hang all three lids.
  9. Fit-out. Punnet rack screwed through into the bay-03 studs, hanging rail flanges under the front plate in bay 04, root trough with its stop-ends siliconed and tested for leaks, shelf lip rail, honesty box bolted to the 1200 stud, signs on.
  10. Paint. Primer plus two coats, end grain first. Leave it a fortnight before you seal the return box's rubber against fresh paint.
  11. Soft open. One weekend with eggs and one other line only. Watch what people actually stop for, then load the bays properly.

The detail everyone skips

Seal every cut end and every drilled hole, on the treated timber and the ply both. Treatment does not penetrate to the core, so a raw cut end standing in a puddle is the one place a stand like this fails, and it fails from the inside where you cannot see it coming.

10Compliance

Before you open

Selling your own eggs and produce at your own gate is deliberately easy in New Zealand — but the detail sits with MPI and your council, and it does change. Treat everything below as the list of things to confirm, not as the answer.

Confirm these yourself

Rules and thresholds shift, and yours may differ by district. Ring MPI about the eggs and your district council about the stall and the sign before you open. Both calls are short, free, and much easier than being asked to shut down in your second week.

Eggs

  • MPI has long allowed small flocks selling direct to consumers at the gate to operate without a registered risk management programme — the commonly cited line is 100 or fewer laying hens. Confirm the current threshold and what it requires of you.
  • Never sell cracked, leaking or visibly dirty eggs. This is the one rule with no nuance in it.
  • Do not wash them. Washing strips the bloom that keeps bacteria out. Wipe a lightly marked egg dry; discard a badly soiled one.
  • Label each carton with your name, your address and the pack date, even where you are not strictly required to. It is good practice, it builds trust, and it makes a traceback trivial if you ever need one.
  • Reused cartons must not carry another producer's branding in a way that misleads the buyer. Your own label over the top solves it.

Fruit and vegetables

  • Whole, unprocessed produce sold by the grower generally sits outside the Food Act's registration requirements. The moment you cut, cook, bag as a prepared salad, or preserve anything, you are in different territory — check first.
  • Sell your own produce. Reselling someone else's changes what you are, both legally and to the neighbours.

The road and the sign

  • The berm is usually road reserve. A formed pull-off, and often a roadside sign, needs council approval; on a state highway it needs NZTA's.
  • Some district plans treat a permanent roadside stall as a structure or as commercial activity in a rural zone. Ask before you concrete anything in.
  • Sightlines both ways matter more than any of it. If a car cannot stop safely, the stand is in the wrong place regardless of what the rules permit.

Money and cover

  • Gate takings are income. Keep a tally book in the stand; it takes ten seconds a day and saves a bad afternoon in April.
  • Tell your insurer. A structure at your boundary that the public uses is worth a sentence on the policy, and public liability cover is usually cheap to add.
11Operating

Running it

The stand is a fortnight's work. What decides whether it is still going next summer is the rhythm you put around it.

Price so nobody needs change

Whole dollars, ideally $5 / $8 / $10, and no odd cents anywhere on the board. It removes the float, it removes the coin dish, and it removes the customer who drives off because they only had a twenty. Hand-letter the prices on a board rather than printing them — painted prices read as a farm, printed ones read as a shop.

Restock twice, not once

Morning and late afternoon in summer, once a day the rest of the year. Every restock: pull anything tired, empty the carton return, empty the honesty box, top up the flower buckets, write the day's takings in the book. Ten minutes, and it is the difference between a stand people check and one they drive past.

Never leave it empty-looking

A half-empty bay reads as closed from a moving car. If a line runs out, spread the rest across the bench and put a jar of flowers where the gap is. Keep one thing at eye level in every bay at all times.

Signs to paint

Road sign

EGGS · FLOWERS · VEG
Big, high-contrast, one line, readable at 80 km/h. Still not a list — twelve vegetables on a roadside sign reads as nothing at all.

On the back wall

Everything here is grown or laid on this farm.
One sentence. It is the whole pitch.

By the box

On the hatches

What to change after the first season

  • Watch which bay empties first and give it more room. The four-bay split is a starting hypothesis, not a finding — if the punnets outrun everything, the rack wants a fourth tier or a second bay.
  • Count the carton returns. If the rate is under about a third, the incentive is too small or the box is not obvious enough — usually the second.
  • If cash keeps beating transfers, your account sign is too small or too far from the goods. Move it to eye level directly above the egg well.
  • Add a chalkboard for what is new this week. It is the cheapest thing you can do to make regulars stop again.
